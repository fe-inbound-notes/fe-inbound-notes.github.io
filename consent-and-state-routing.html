Consent, Licensed States, and Call Routing for Final Expense Agents

Inbound calls feel simpler than outbound dialing. The person placed the call. You did not reach into a list and press their number. That does not mean compliance is someone else's problem, and it does not mean every call that rings is one you are allowed to finish.

Two issues show up again and again for agents who take final expense inbound calls: how the caller got into the campaign in the first place, and whether you are licensed in the state that matters for the sale. This article is a practical briefing, not legal advice. Rules change, and your agency, your carrier, and your own compliance contact have the last word. Use this to know what to ask and what to refuse.

Know how the caller got to you

Most inbound campaigns start with an advertisement, a landing page, or a phone number in a commercial. The vendor should be able to tell you, in plain language, how they obtain consent to market to the people who see that offer, and how they handle someone who asks not to be contacted again.

Ask for that explanation before you take a large number of calls, not after a complaint. Useful questions include:

  • What does the person see or hear before they dial?
  • How is consent recorded, and for which products?
  • What happens when a caller says they did not request this, or they want to be taken off the list?
  • If you need to call the person back, whose number shows up, and is that callback covered by the same consent?

Write down the answers you get. If a vendor cannot explain their process, treat that as a reason to pause.

When you place a callback, stick to the time you agreed on and the number the person confirmed. If they ask you not to call again, stop and record it the way your shop requires. Inbound intent on Monday does not give you a free pass to keep calling on Friday after they have told you to quit.

If your state or your carrier requires specific disclosures at the start of a sale, keep those in your opening notes. Doing the disclosure late, or not at all, is a problem you cannot fix by blaming the vendor.

Route only the states you can actually serve

You may take a call only when you are properly licensed for the work, and final expense is still life insurance. The relevant state is usually the state where the applicant lives, not the state you sit in while you answer, and not the state a person happens to be visiting. Confirm residency early. "What state do you live in?" belongs near the top of the call, right after you know who the coverage is for.

Set your vendor routing so you are open only in states where your license is active and you are appointed with the companies you plan to offer. If a license is pending, expired, or limited, do not leave that state turned on and hope you can "just talk." Talking is still part of the sale.

When a call slips through from a state you cannot serve, be direct. Tell the person you are not able to help in their state, apologize, and end the call without a quote, an informal ballpark, or a suggestion that they apply anyway. A ballpark number is still advice. If your shop can transfer that caller to an agent who is licensed there, use it. If not, do not improvise a referral unless your agency has a rule for that.

People travel. A caller may be away for the winter and still keep a primary residence somewhere else. Do not guess. Ask where they live most of the year and where they want the policy written, then follow carrier rules. If you are unsure, stop and check.

Keep your own records tidy

Review your routing settings, license renewals, and appointment lists on a schedule, not when a call goes wrong. When a license lapses, take that state offline the same day. Keep call notes that show you asked for the state, the residency answer you were given, and any request to stop contact. Those notes help in a later review, and they help you catch a routing problem if calls keep arriving from a state you turned off.

Do not record calls unless you know the consent and notice rules for the states involved and for your own location. Some states require all-party consent to record. If your vendor records by default, ask how they notify the caller and whether you may use those recordings.

What to do when something feels off

If a caller says they never asked for this, that they keep getting calls after a request to stop, or that the ad promised something you cannot offer, do not talk them out of the concern. Take the details, follow your vendor's dispute or suppression process, and tell your agency. Patterns of this kind are a sign the campaign, the creative, or the routing needs a hard look.

If you are ever asked to ignore a state rule, to quote a product you are not appointed to sell, or to keep a person on the line so a call lasts long enough to bill, refuse. Your license is worth more than one conversation.

The short version

Inbound does not mean anything goes. Understand how consent was collected, honor stop requests, and be careful with callbacks. Open your routing only in states where you can legally do the work, confirm residency early, and do not quote where you cannot write. Keep notes, keep your licenses current, and ask for help when a fact pattern is not clear.
